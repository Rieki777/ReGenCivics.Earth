### Block Food Forest
**Tier:** Easy Mode  
**Rewards:** 444 $ReGen  
**Duration:** 1 day event (weeks of coordination)  
**Element:** earth  
**Commitment:** Community organizing and event facilitation

#### Story Card

Your street is underused. The asphalt is hot. The only green is in people's backyards, invisible from the sidewalk. What if one day the whole thing changed?

A block food forest is exactly what it sounds. You organize your neighbors. Each person brings something: potted fruit trees, berry bushes, garden beds, perennials. You coordinate one day when everyone shows up at the same time. Together, you redesign the street corner, the parkway, maybe a few front yards. In eight hours, a conventional block becomes a visible forest garden.

The real magic happens when people walking past stop and ask what you're doing. When kids see fruit trees they can pick from. When neighbors who've never talked before work side by side. This is how people learn that transformation is possible and that they can do it together.

#### What This Quest Involves

**Phase 1: Vision and Inventory.** Walk your block several times. Map out the best planting spots: parkways, corners, unused yards, verges. Talk to neighbors individually. Learn who has what to contribute and what they want to see happen.

**Phase 2: Design.** Sketch a simple plan. Think about sight lines, foot traffic, water access. Plan for a mix: fruit trees, berries, perennials, ground cover. Make it beautiful and functional. Get feedback from neighbors.

**Phase 3: Sourcing.** Call nurseries. Talk to local gardeners. Post on your neighborhood network. Give people a deadline to bring plants and materials. Be clear about what you need.

**Phase 4: The Day.** Host it as an event. Start early. Have water, snacks, tools ready. Guide people on placement. Take lots of photos and video of the work and the reactions. Play music. Make it celebratory.

**Phase 5: Follow-up.** Visit the site weekly for the first month. Water as needed. Document how it looks as plants settle in. Share photos with neighbors. Keep the story alive.

#### Deliverable

A photo and video documentation of the design, the day of work, and the finished block food forest. Include at least three photos showing the before, the action, and the completed planting. Bonus: video of neighbors' reactions and a short written reflection on what changed.

#### What Support Looks Like

- Access to the community network to post about the project and find neighbors to collaborate with
- Plant sourcing help through ReGen Civics connections (local nurseries, seed libraries, plant swaps)
- Design templates and logistics checklists to make organizing easier
- Amplification: we'll feature your block food forest in our newsletter and social media to inspire other neighborhoods

#### Connected To

- Welcome Aboard: "Get to Know Your Block" (introductory quest)
- Food Forest Deep Dive quest series (if you want to expand next season)
- Networked Community Garden (EPIC, next tier up)

### Networked Community Garden
**Tier:** Easy Mode  
**Rewards:** 444 $ReGen  
**Duration:** 3-6 months  
**Element:** earth  
**Commitment:** Weekly coordination and ongoing tending

#### Story Card

Food independence doesn't happen in isolation. It happens when neighbors pool their land and their labor. A networked community garden is simple in concept: each household offers a piece of their property. Together, these pieces form one shared food growing system, governed collectively from the start.

This could be multiple small plots on different properties, connected by raised beds and pathways that neighbors maintain together. Or it could be one vacant lot transformed into garden beds that serve several families. The key is that you're designing the shared governance, the labor-sharing, the harvest-sharing as integral parts of the system, not afterthoughts.

When this works, something shifts. Neighbors show up weekly. They know each other's growing styles, what each family needs, what the soil is doing. Food production becomes a rhythm that holds the neighborhood together.

#### What This Quest Involves

**Phase 1: Coalition Building.** Identify 5-10 neighbors who want to participate. Meet informally. Learn what they want to grow, how much time they can give, what their land can offer. Build trust before making commitments.

**Phase 2: Land Mapping and Site Selection.** Visit each potential growing space. Assess sunlight, water access, soil condition. Decide on one primary site or multiple connected plots. Document the baseline conditions.

**Phase 3: Governance Design.** Before you plant anything, design the shared agreements: How will you make decisions? How will labor be divided? How will you harvest and distribute food? What happens if someone can't participate that season? Write these agreements down and refine them with the group.

**Phase 4: Site Preparation and Planting.** Prepare the soil. Build infrastructure (raised beds, pathways, water systems, tool storage). Create a planting calendar that makes sense for your region and your group's rhythm. Plant together.

**Phase 5: Seasonal Tending and Adaptation.** Show up weekly. Share the work. Harvest together. At the end of the season, gather to reflect on what worked, what didn't, and what you'd do differently next year.

#### Deliverable

A governance document (1-2 pages, plain language) that describes your coalition, the land, the agreements you made, and the planting plan. Include photos of each site from start to harvest. Document one full season of participation and growth. Include a reflection on what the community learned about shared food production.

#### What Support Looks Like

- Governance templates and examples from other networked gardens
- Seed sourcing and soil amendment connections through our network
- Monthly check-ins with other teams doing networked gardens (learning calls)
- If your garden thrives, we'll feature it as a model for other neighborhoods

#### Connected To

- Block Food Forest (complementary EASY quest)
- Cornfield to Cloud Forest (scaling up to a larger land project)
- Bioregional Currency Launch (could run parallel to create a food economy)

### Bioregional Currency Launch
**Tier:** Easy Mode  
**Rewards:** 444 $ReGen  
**Element:** water  
**Duration:** 2-3 months  
**Commitment:** Design, coordination, and launch event

#### Story Card

Money is a story that we agree to believe. Most of us believe the story told by banks and governments. But what if your neighborhood told a different story? A story about local abundance, skill-sharing, and mutual aid? That's what a bioregional currency is.

You design it yourself. You decide what it represents. You create the physical or digital tokens. You host a launch party where people start using it. You document how the currency circulates, what people trade, how it changes the relationships in your neighborhood.

This is not about replacing dollars. It's about creating an additional layer of exchange that keeps value circulating locally and builds relationships. It's about reclaiming the power to tell stories about what has value.

#### What This Quest Involves

**Phase 1: Research and Design.** Learn how other currencies have been designed. What do they call themselves? What do they value? How is trust established? Spend time understanding your bioregion: what's abundant, what's needed, what skills do people have. Design your currency around that reality.

**Phase 2: Community Feedback.** Share your design with neighbors and potential participants. Listen. Refine. Do people understand it? Are they excited? Build genuine buy-in before you commit to launch.

**Phase 3: Production.** Create the physical currency (printed notes, coins, tokens) or set up the digital infrastructure. Be thoughtful about design. Make it beautiful and meaningful. Use artwork and symbols that tell your story.

**Phase 4: Launch Event.** Host a celebration. Invite people to exchange dollars for your currency. Explain the vision. Get initial transactions happening. Make it festive. Create the first stories of exchange.

**Phase 5: Documentation and Community Building.** Track and share stories of how the currency is being used. Who traded what? What happened because of it? Iterate based on feedback. Build a small team to steward it through the first season.

#### Deliverable

A currency design document (2-3 pages) that explains your bioregion, the currency's name and symbols, how it works, and the governance for how new currency is created and circulated. Include photos of the currency design and the launch event. Document at least 10 real transactions with stories of what was exchanged and what it meant.

#### What Support Looks Like

- Connections to SEEDS and LocalScale, who support bioregional currency design through the Rainbow Seeds Protocol
- Design templates and examples from functioning currencies in the regen network
- Technical setup support for digital currencies (if you're going that route)
- Community amplification: we'll feature your currency launch in our network and connect you with other currency projects

#### Connected To

- Networked Community Garden (economic layer for food system)
- HOA to Village (currency is often part of village governance)
- Global network of bioregional currencies and local economies

### Cornfield to Cloud Forest
**Tier:** Hard Mode  
**Rewards:** 777 $ReGen  
**Element:** earth  
**Duration:** 1-3 years  
**Commitment:** Active project management and regular site work

#### Story Card

Conventional monoculture agriculture has stripped the soil of life. The land is tired. It's been productive in a linear way, but it hasn't been regenerating. You want to change that.

A food forest is a designed ecosystem that mimics nature's abundance. Instead of one crop, you have many. Instead of bare soil, you have layered growth from canopy trees down to ground cover. Instead of external inputs, you build soil fertility and water retention. It takes years to establish, but once it does, it feeds people and builds fertility at the same time.

This is one of the most visible and measurable transformations you can do. You can document soil health before and after. You can count species. You can measure yields. You can show that a different agriculture is possible, right here, in the middle of farmland.

#### What This Quest Involves

**Phase 1: Baseline Assessment.** Soil testing. Water mapping. Existing vegetation survey. Talk to neighbors and the landowner about their vision. Understand what you're starting with. Document everything with photos and data.

**Phase 2: Design.** Work with a permaculture designer or use open design resources. Map the forest layers: tall trees, understory trees, shrubs, herbaceous plants, ground cover, root crops. Plan for diverse food production: fruits, nuts, berries, leafy greens, roots. Think about water flows, wind patterns, microclimates.

**Phase 3: Infrastructure.** Install water systems if needed. Build soil through mulching, compost, cover crops. Create paths and working areas. This phase can take several months. Document your methods.

**Phase 4: Planting.** Work with community volunteers if possible. Plant in phases over multiple seasons. Start with trees (they're the long game). Add understory and shrubs. Document each planting event. Track what you planted where and when.

**Phase 5: Tending and Monitoring.** Show up regularly. Weed, water, mulch, observe. Keep detailed records of growth, challenges, pest patterns, yields. At the end of each season, reflect and adjust your plan. After 3-5 years, the forest should be substantially established and producing food.

#### Deliverable

A complete design document with maps, species list, and timeline. Baseline data: soil tests, species inventory, photos of the original field. Ongoing documentation: monthly or seasonal photos from fixed vantage points. Year-end reflection reports for each year of the project. Final assessment: soil health improvement, biodiversity gain, food produced, lessons learned.

#### What Support Looks Like

- Connection to permaculture designers and food forest specialists in our network
- Soil testing and remediation guidance
- Seedling and tree sourcing at scale
- Project management structure and learning calls with other food forest teams
- Potential funding for land access or major infrastructure

#### Connected To

- Block Food Forest and Networked Community Garden (scaled to agricultural land)
- Pasture to Paradise (working with different baseline conditions)
- Retreat Center (could use food forest as part of a larger project)

### Pasture to Paradise
**Tier:** Hard Mode  
**Rewards:** 777 $ReGen  
**Element:** earth  
**Duration:** Multi-year  
**Commitment:** Sustained active land stewardship

#### Story Card

Degraded pasture land tells a story of extraction. Overgrazing, monoculture, chemical inputs, no rest. The soil is compacted. The diversity is gone. The land feels tired and barren, even when it's technically still producing something.

You're going to change that story. You're going to transform this land into a thriving food forest and wildlife habitat that regenerates soil, sequesters carbon, builds biodiversity, and produces abundance. This is not a quick project. This is a multi-year commitment to learning how to work with the land and letting the land teach you.

This is the kind of transformation that moves people. When someone sees the before and after, they understand that a different future is possible. They see that degraded land can become alive again.

#### What This Quest Involves

**Phase 1: Deep Assessment.** Spend time on the land. Walk it in different seasons. Soil testing, water mapping, existing plant and animal inventory. Interview previous owners. Understand what created the degradation and what the land wants to be.

**Phase 2: Regenerative Grazing Plan (if applicable).** If you're keeping animals, design a rotation system that builds soil instead of depleting it. Plan for adequate recovery time. Document the grazing plan and the reasoning behind it.

**Phase 3: Diversity Planting.** Introduce perennial forage species, fruit and nut trees, nitrogen-fixing plants, deep-rooted species. Build soil through strategic cover cropping and mulching. This phase overlaps with grazing (if applicable) and takes 2-3 years.

**Phase 4: Wildlife and Water Infrastructure.** Build ponds, wetlands, or water features to support wildlife. Plant native species that provide food and habitat. Create hedgerows and edges that wildlife can move through. Establish no-till or low-disturbance practices that allow soil life to recover.

**Phase 5: Long-term Stewardship and Documentation.** Year after year, you observe and adjust. You document soil improvement, biodiversity increase, carbon sequestration. You harvest and produce food. You build relationships with the land and with others who support the work.

#### Deliverable

A comprehensive transformation document including baseline assessments (soil data, species inventory, photos), a long-term stewardship plan, and annual reports for at least 3 years of active work. Include evidence of soil improvement (soil tests, organic matter increase), biodiversity gain (species counts, photos), and production (food and fiber harvested). Tell the story of how the land and the community changed together.

#### What Support Looks Like

- Connections to regenerative agriculture experts and land stewards in the network
- Soil biology and pasture management training
- Animal grazing planning support (if applicable)
- Long-term learning partnership with other land projects at similar scale
- Potential for inclusion in a bioregional land network and funding pathways

#### Connected To

- Cornfield to Cloud Forest (different starting conditions, similar transformation arc)
- Startup Town (could be part of a larger land settlement project)
- Retreat Center (could serve as a base for work on the land)

### HOA to Village
**Tier:** Hard Mode  
**Rewards:** 777 $ReGen  
**Element:** fire  
**Duration:** 1-2 years  
**Commitment:** Community organizing and governance facilitation

#### Story Card

A homeowners association is a legal structure designed to maintain property values and enforce rules. It's often experienced as a source of friction, not connection.

What if you transformed it into something different? A village. A place where people know each other and choose to share resources. Where there's a shared food system, shared tools, shared childcare or elder care. Where decisions are made together and everyone has voice. Where it still maintains basic infrastructure, but the spirit is abundance and mutual aid instead of control and enforcement.

This is governance transformation. It's about working within existing legal structures while fundamentally changing their purpose and how they operate. It's slow, political work. It's also the work that creates the conditions for everything else to flourish.

#### What This Quest Involves

**Phase 1: Vision and Listening.** Host conversations with neighbors about what they actually want. Not what the HOA tells them they should want, but what would make their daily lives better. Listen for shared desires: food security, connection, time, support.

**Phase 2: Governance Design.** Create new bylaws and operating agreements that shift power from enforcement to facilitation. Design how decisions will be made. How will resources be shared? What agreements do you need about property use, shared growing spaces, tool libraries? Write these down clearly.

**Phase 3: Shared Infrastructure.** Design and build the physical systems that support the vision. Community gardens, a tool library, a gathering space, a bulletin board or online platform for coordination. Start small. Let people see what's possible.

**Phase 4: Transition Process.** Work with the existing HOA structure. You might need to elect new leadership or advocate for bylaw changes. This phase requires patience and political skill. Document the process and the resistance you encounter and how you address it.

**Phase 5: Community Building and Anchoring.** Once the infrastructure and governance are in place, focus on building the culture. Regular gatherings. Shared meals. Celebration of milestones. Make the village feel real and worth participating in.

#### Deliverable

A governance document that shows the old HOA structure and the new village structure. Include bylaws, operating agreements, and decision-making processes. Document the community input process and what you heard. Include a map or photos of shared infrastructure. Tell the story of the transformation process, including challenges and how you worked through them.

#### What Support Looks Like

- Templates and examples of village governance structures from other HOAs
- Legal and bylaw review support
- Facilitation training for governance meetings
- Community organizing coaching
- Connections to other HOA transformation projects for mutual learning

#### Connected To

- Apartment Building Community (similar governance transformation, different setting)
- Networked Community Garden or Block Food Forest (physical expression of the village)
- Bioregional Currency (economic layer of village life)

### Retreat Center
**Tier:** Hard Mode  
**Rewards:** 777 $ReGen  
**Element:** earth  
**Duration:** 1-2 years  
**Commitment:** Project management and hands-on building

#### Story Card

A retreat center is a place where people come to slow down, to do deep work, to heal, to learn, to be held by community. It's a base camp for the transformation you're doing on the land. It's where people gather for ceremonies, workshops, collective reflection. It's infrastructure for the culture we're building.

Building a retreat center requires vision, project management, resources, and coordination. You're creating a physical space that holds the values of your land project and your community. The design matters. The materials matter. The process of building it together matters as much as the finished building.

This is one of the most grounding and embodied forms of work you can do. You're literally building the future with your hands.

#### What This Quest Involves

**Phase 1: Visioning and Design.** What do you want this center to be? Who will use it? What will happen there? Work with a designer or use participatory design processes with your community. Think about infrastructure: water, power, sleeping spaces, kitchen, gathering spaces, outdoor areas. Create a detailed design and layout.

**Phase 2: Site Selection and Preparation.** Choose the location on or near your land project. Prepare the site: clearing, leveling, water and power infrastructure. Get permitting done. Understand your constraints and opportunities.

**Phase 3: Materials and Sourcing.** Source materials, preferably local and regenerative. Work with local builders or skilled volunteers. Plan a building schedule that works with seasons and people's availability. Create a budget and identify funding.

**Phase 4: Building.** Do the work. This could be a series of build weekends with volunteers, or a longer construction phase with hired builders, or some combination. Document the process. Build with intention. Involve community members in the physical work.

**Phase 5: Opening and Use.** Launch the retreat center. Host your first gathering. Learn how it works. Refine based on use. Build a stewardship structure for ongoing maintenance and use.

#### Deliverable

A design document with vision, site plans, materials list, and budget. Before and after photos showing site preparation and construction. A photo and narrative documentation of the building process, ideally showing community involvement. Opening event photos and a reflection on what the center enables. A first-year use report showing who used it, what happened there, and lessons learned.

#### What Support Looks Like

- Design support and connections to regenerative builders and architects
- Permitting and regulatory navigation help
- Material sourcing and bulk purchasing for building teams
- Fundraising strategy for retreat center development
- Connection to a network of other retreat centers and learning communities

#### Connected To

- Cornfield to Cloud Forest, Pasture to Paradise, or Startup Town (the center serves these land projects)
- Regenerative building and natural building skill networks
- Spiritual and ceremony communities who will use the center

### Golf Course Transformation
**Tier:** Hard Mode  
**Rewards:** 777 $ReGen  
**Element:** earth  
**Duration:** 2-5 years  
**Commitment:** Coalition building, ongoing advocacy, and land design

#### Story Card

Golf courses occupy vast tracts of land in and around cities. They're often publicly visible, well-maintained, and accessible. They're also monoculture wastelands sprayed with pesticides and herbicides, using enormous amounts of water, serving a small population of mostly affluent people.

Imagine transforming that land into a food forest or a wildlife corridor or a community land project. Imagine the visibility of that transformation. Imagine the conversations it would spark.

This is a big project. It requires coalition building with the city, with community groups, with environmental organizations. It requires navigating politics and competing interests. It requires a compelling vision for what could happen instead. But it's possible. And when it works, it shifts what's possible at the city scale.

#### What This Quest Involves

**Phase 1: Site Selection and Opportunity Mapping.** Identify a golf course that might be receptive or might be vulnerable. Research its ownership, its history, its current use and economics. Talk to the city. Talk to environmental groups. Understand the landscape of possibility.

**Phase 2: Coalition Building.** Bring together a diverse coalition: neighbors who want access to the land, environmental organizations, food security advocates, indigenous communities (if relevant), city officials who see the opportunity. Build shared vision. Listen to different priorities and find common ground.

**Phase 3: Alternative Vision Development.** Design what could happen on this land. Food forest? Wildlife habitat? Community growing space? Housing? Some combination? Make it compelling. Make it beautiful. Make it seem real and possible.

**Phase 4: Political Process.** This is advocacy work. Meetings with city council, planning commissions, the golf course owner. Public hearings. Letters and documentation. Building public support. This phase is often longer than the land work itself.

**Phase 5: Transition and Implementation.** If you succeed (or partially succeed), you move into designing and building the new land use. This could take years. You stay involved in ensuring the vision is realized.

#### Deliverable

A transformation proposal document including site analysis, coalition membership and input, alternative vision with maps and renderings, environmental benefits analysis (carbon sequestration, water infiltration, habitat potential), and economic analysis (cost-benefit compared to golf course maintenance). Document the political process: meetings, public input, decisions made. If transformation happens, document the implementation and first-year outcomes.

#### What Support Looks Like

- Coalition facilitation and strategy support
- Connections to city planning advocates and environmental organizations
- Expertise in alternative land use design and community land projects
- Public communication and campaign support
- Potential funding for advocacy or initial land improvements

#### Connected To

- Bioregional land use strategy and citywide transformation initiatives
- Environmental justice and land access movements
- Cornfield to Cloud Forest and Pasture to Paradise (ecological design approaches)

### Apartment Building Community
**Tier:** Hard Mode  
**Rewards:** 777 $ReGen  
**Element:** fire  
**Duration:** 6-12 months  
**Commitment:** Community facilitation and ongoing coordination

#### Story Card

Apartment buildings are often sites of isolation. People live stacked on top of each other and rarely speak. There's no shared ownership, limited sense of collective identity, no mechanisms for mutual support.

You can change that. You can organize the tenants or residents of a building to create shared food growing spaces, shared tools and equipment, shared childcare or elder care, collective decision-making on things that affect everyone. You can transform a building into a regenerative living community.

This requires facilitation skill and deep commitment, but it's possible. And it's powerful. It's one of the highest-density forms of community building and regeneration you can do.

#### What This Quest Involves

**Phase 1: Assessment and Coalition.** Map out your building or complex. Who are the residents? What languages do they speak? What do they need? What resources do they have? Have preliminary conversations. Start building trust and identifying who wants to organize.

**Phase 2: Governance Design.** Work with interested residents to create agreements and structures for shared decision-making. How will decisions be made? What's shared and what's individual? What are everyone's responsibilities? Write this down. Make it clear and accessible.

**Phase 3: Shared Growing Space.** Identify and design growing space: rooftop, courtyard, individual balconies connected into a network. Plan what you'll grow and how you'll tend it together. Create a labor-sharing and harvest-sharing plan.

**Phase 4: Shared Infrastructure and Services.** Beyond food, what else could be shared? Tool libraries, a shared pantry, a community bulletin board or chat, childcare or elder care coordination, skill-sharing. Start with one or two things and expand.

**Phase 5: Community Culture.** Build regular gathering spaces and rhythms. Shared meals, community meetings, celebrations. Make the community feel real and worth participating in. Document how relationships have changed.

#### Deliverable

A community agreement document that shows governance, shared growing plans, and other shared infrastructure. Include photos of shared spaces before transformation and after. Document resident participation in design and decision-making. Tell the story of relationship change: friendships, mutual aid, conflicts navigated. Include a reflection from multiple residents about what changed.

#### What Support Looks Like

- Community organizing and facilitation coaching
- Governance templates and examples
- Rooftop or small-space growing guidance
- Connections to other apartment community projects
- Regular check-ins and learning with other teams doing building transformation

#### Connected To

- HOA to Village (similar governance transformation at building scale)
- Bioregional Currency (economic layer for community exchange)
- Block Food Forest or Networked Community Garden (growing at smaller scale)

## EXPERT MODE QUEST

### Startup Town
**Tier:** Expert Mode  
**Rewards:** 1444 $ReGen  
**Element:** earth  
**Duration:** 5+ years  
**Commitment:** Life-stage commitment and full community leadership

#### Story Card

This is the long game. You're building a new settlement from scratch. Land, governance, housing, food systems, energy systems, waste systems, water systems, cultural life. All of it designed from the ground up on regenerative principles.

The Ringing Cedars movement in Russia did this. Communities across North America are doing it now. You design the settlement to be beautiful, to work well, to regenerate the land it sits on, and to embody the values and culture of the people who live there.

This is not a business project. It's a life project. You're building the future that you want to live in, and inviting others to build it with you. The work takes years. It requires resources, vision, resilience, and a community that's committed to the long arc.

But when it works, you've created something that will seed transformation for generations. You've shown that a different way of living is possible.

#### What This Quest Involves

**Phase 1: Vision and Land Search.** Get clear on your vision for the settlement. What are the core values? What's the population size you're aiming for? What's the climate and bioregion? What kind of land do you need? Search for land. This could take months or years.

**Phase 2: Land Acquisition and Baseline Assessment.** Secure land access through purchase, lease, or community arrangement. Do deep baseline assessment: ecology, water, soil, existing use. Map existing assets and constraints. Document the current state thoroughly.

**Phase 3: Governance Design.** This is foundational work. How will the settlement make decisions? What's individual and what's collective? How are children raised? How are elders cared for? How are resources shared? How are conflicts resolved? Build this design with the founding community. Write it down. Refine it.

**Phase 4: Infrastructure Planning and Phased Development.** Design all systems: water (collection, treatment, distribution), food (growing space, storage, processing), energy (generation, storage, distribution), waste (composting, recycling, processing), buildings (housing, gathering spaces, work spaces), transportation. Plan how you'll build these in phases. Year one might focus on water and a central gathering space. Year two might add housing and food infrastructure. Plan 5-10 years out.

**Phase 5: Community Building and Inhabitation.** Launch the settlement. People move in. The work begins. You build houses, you tend land, you solve conflicts, you refine systems, you celebrate milestones. You document how the community is developing. You stay in the project for the long term.

#### Deliverable

A comprehensive settlement design document including: vision statement and values, land and site assessment, governance structures and agreements, detailed system designs (water, food, energy, waste, housing, transportation), phased development plan with timeline and budget, and community composition and decision-making processes.

Ongoing documentation (5+ year arc): Before photos and descriptions of the land. Quarterly or annual development reports showing progress on each system. Photos of construction and inhabitation. Stories from residents about how living there is changing them. Long-term ecological monitoring (soil, water, biodiversity). Final reflection: what worked, what didn't, what you'd do differently, what you've learned.

#### What Support Looks Like

- Deep partnership with the ReGen Civics community and broader regen network
- Connections to other intentional communities and settlement projects for learning and mutual support
- Governance facilitation and organizational development coaching
- Funding and grant pathways for land acquisition and infrastructure development
- Technical expertise in regenerative systems design (water, food, energy, buildings)
- Regular learning calls with founding teams of other settlements
- Amplification and documentation support for your story as a seed for others

#### Connected To

- Global network of intentional communities and regenerative settlements
- All other EPIC quests as potential components of the settlement
- The broader Ringing Cedars movement and regenerative renaissance
